Status of Pesticide Pollution in Soils of India

Causes, consequences and solutions. India applies less pesticide per hectare than many countries, yet old, persistent chemicals still linger in its soils and new pressure keeps building.

Pesticide pollution in soil is the build-up of pesticide chemicals and their breakdown products in agricultural and non-agricultural land. Soil is the final landing place for much of what is sprayed: residues bind to soil organic matter, move with rainwater and irrigation, and can reach rivers and groundwater.[4]

An ICAR review concludes that pesticide use is unavoidable for intensive Indian agriculture, but that a weak legal framework and low farmer awareness have led to pollution of soils and sediments. It also notes that the contamination found today does not mirror current use, and points to the continued use of banned pesticides.[4]

Background and history

Pesticide production in India began in 1952. By 2023 output had reached about 258,000 tonnes and the industry was valued at around ₹500 billion.[10] Early organochlorines such as DDT and HCH were used both on crops and for disease-vector control, and they left a lasting chemical signature in soil.[5]

  • 1952 Domestic pesticide production begins.[10]
  • 1968 The Insecticides Act becomes the main law governing pesticides; the Insecticides Rules follow in 1971.[10]
  • 1976 Aerial spraying of endosulfan begins on cashew estates in Kasaragod, Kerala.[14]
  • 2011 Endosulfan production and distribution are banned by the Supreme Court.[14]
  • 2015 The Soil Health Card Scheme is launched on 19 February at Suratgarh, Rajasthan.[15]
  • 2017 On 10 January the Supreme Court orders ₹5 lakh compensation per endosulfan victim.[13]
  • 2020 A Pesticide Management Bill is introduced in the Rajya Sabha (March).[10]
  • 2022 In Baiju K.G. v. Dr V.P. Joy (13 May) the Court directs monthly monitoring of compensation.[13]
  • 2024 The National Mission on Natural Farming is approved (25 Nov).[15]
  • 2026 The agriculture ministry releases a draft Pesticides Management Bill to replace the 1968 Act.[10]

Causes

Persistence of legacy chemicals

Organochlorines bind to soil organic carbon and break down slowly. Studies in the Himalaya and along the south-west coast found that organic carbon helps hold these compounds in soil, and that metabolites such as DDE, DDD and endosulfan sulfate point to past use.[6][7]

Continued use of banned or restricted products

The ICAR review highlights widespread use of banned pesticides.[4] On the south-west coast, traces of technical HCH at specific sites indicated ongoing use despite the agricultural ban.[7] In the Himalaya, ratios of DDT metabolites suggested recent application of technical DDT and dicofol.[6]

Weak regulation and low awareness

The 1968 Insecticides Act is still the governing law, and critics say enforcement and modernisation have lagged. Limited farmer knowledge of safe use and of alternatives such as integrated pest management is a recurring concern.[4][10]

Transport from where it is applied

Runoff and spray drift carry pesticides beyond the treated field into neighbouring soils and water bodies.[4]

Intensive cropping

Use is concentrated in a few states and crops. Chemical pesticide consumption is highest in Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Punjab, Telangana and West Bengal, and the Indo-Gangetic plains show above-average toxicity pressure globally.[2][11]

Scale of use

The Directorate of Plant Protection, Quarantine & Storage reports that chemical pesticide use rose by about 20% over a decade.[1]

Reading the numbers. India's average per-hectare use is low, and a 2023 parliamentary committee noted consumption of only 0.5 kg per hectare against 17 kg in some other countries.[10] National averages can hide local hotspots, which is where contamination studies keep finding residues.

Why old chemicals still matter. The north-India study found HCH dominant, and ratios consistent with past use of technical DDT. The ICAR review adds that contamination levels do not reflect present usage, meaning soil carries a historical burden that outlasts bans.[4][5]

Consequences

Soil life and fertility

Pesticides alter soil microbial communities, which can reduce fertility and affect yields.[4] A global study published in Science (2013-2019 data, 201 countries) found that toxicity pressure on soil organisms rose about 4.6% a year and on terrestrial arthropods about 6.4% a year. India, Brazil, China and the US together contribute 53-68% of global pesticide toxicity, and levels in India exceed the global average in the Indo-Gangetic plains.[11]

Water

Residues leach and run off into groundwater and surface water. Near Hisar, about 80% of tube-well samples exceeded regulatory limits, and in Thiruvallur a borewell held endosulfan sulfate at 15.9 µg/L.[8][9]

Food

Data from the ICAR All India Network Project on Pesticide Residues show that residues were detected in 28% of food samples analysed between 2018 and 2023.[10]

Human health: the Kasaragod endosulfan case

From 1976, endosulfan was sprayed from the air over cashew plantations owned by the Plantation Corporation of Kerala in Kasaragod district. Residents of 15 villages developed physical and mental disorders.[14] The case is often compared to the Bhopal gas tragedy.[10]

Courts

The Supreme Court banned endosulfan production and distribution in 2011. On 10 January 2017 it directed compensation of ₹5 lakh to each victim. In 2022, in Baiju K.G. v. Dr V.P. Joy (2022 INSC 575), the bench observed that Kerala had done virtually nothing for five years, awarded ₹50,000 costs to each of the eight petitioners, and directed the Chief Secretary to hold monthly meetings to identify victims, disburse compensation and provide medical facilities near their homes.[13] Earlier in 2022 the state's own records showed 2,966 of 6,727 victims still unpaid.[14]

Law and governance

Pesticides are regulated under the Insecticides Act, 1968 and the Insecticides Rules, 1971. A Pesticide Management Bill was introduced in the Rajya Sabha in March 2020, and the agriculture ministry has since released a draft Pesticides Management Bill to replace the 1968 law.[10]

Critics quoted by Mongabay India argue that the draft modernises regulation but does not do enough to protect people, and that pesticide management in India does not account for biodiversity harm.[10][11]

Solutions

1. Measure what is in the soil

The Soil Health Card scheme, launched in 2015, has delivered over 25 crore cards (as of July 2025). Each card reports 12 parameters: pH, electrical conductivity, organic carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, sulphur, zinc, boron, iron, manganese and copper. Pesticide residues are not among them, so routine residue monitoring remains a gap.[15]

2. Update and enforce the law

Replace the 1968 Act with a modern law that explicitly covers soil and biodiversity, and enforce bans on products such as technical HCH and DDT.[4][10]

3. Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

IPM is treated as the cardinal principle of plant protection policy, and the Government of India has established 31 Central IPM Centres that educate farmers.[16] One review puts biopesticides at only about 8% of consumption, so scale is the challenge.[17]

4. Natural and chemical-free farming

The National Mission on Natural Farming, approved on 25 November 2024 with an outlay of ₹2,481 crore (₹1,584 crore from the Centre and ₹897 crore from states) to 2025-26, targets 1 crore farmers, 15,000 clusters in gram panchayats and 7.5 lakh hectares, supported by Bio-input Resource Centres and certification.[15]

5. Farmer awareness and safe handling

Training on correct dose, timing and disposal tackles overspraying, one of the causes the ICAR review identifies.[4]

Gaps and limitations

  • No national soil survey. Findings come from scattered regional studies with different methods, so values are not directly comparable.[4]
  • Several studies are old or small. The Hisar work sampled 12 soil and 12 water samples in 2002-03; the north-India study was published in 2011.[5][8]
  • Levels are not always alarming. The north-India authors judged levels below Canadian guidelines, while the Himalayan study found moderate to severe ecological risk at some sites. Both findings stand.[5][6]
  • Secondary reporting. Several national figures are reported through news outlets citing ministry or journal data. Check the original records before formal use.
  • Targets change. The natural-farming target is given as 7.5 lakh hectares by PMINDIA; other summaries mention 5 lakh. Bill status may also have moved since early 2026.[15]
